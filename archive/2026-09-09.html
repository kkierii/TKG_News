OpenAI says an internal model resolved the Navier–Stokes Millennium Prize problem
OpenAI · 8 September 2026
OpenAI published a writeup and a Lean formalization on 8 September claiming that an internal, unreleased model produced a proof that the three-dimensional incompressible Navier–Stokes equations can develop a singularity in finite time. The company says the result establishes statements “C” and “D” of the Clay Mathematics Institute’s official formulation — a disproof of global smoothness rather than a proof of it — for a fluid that starts at rest under a smooth applied force with finite energy throughout.
The effort ran on a system of coordinating agents powered by a model OpenAI describes as “significantly more capable than GPT‑6 Astra,” which it says it has been training since 28 August. The group that produced the result involved on the order of 10,000 concurrent agents and reached its resolution on Saturday 5 September, about 88 hours after launch; Lean formalization and verification took a further 17 hours via GPT‑6 Astra. Across all problems attempted the agents sent 4.9 million messages and used roughly 300 billion output tokens, of which 2.7 million messages and about 130 billion output tokens went to Navier–Stokes.
A separate group of nearly 100 agents working about 50 hours produced a disproof of regularity for the unforced Euler equations, which OpenAI says it used to seed the Navier–Stokes effort. OpenAI says it began on 1 September after hearing a rumour it later connected to Levent Alpöge of Anthropic and Tristan Buckmaster of NYU, who it says resolved the forced Euler problem; OpenAI recognises their priority on that result. The proof and the Lean code are public, and OpenAI says it does not intend to claim the $1 million prize. Nothing here has been through peer review, and the Clay Institute’s own rules require publication and a two-year waiting period before a prize is considered.

AI Security
NSA, CISA and FBI name six China-based AI firms over industrial-scale distillation
National Security Agency · 8 September 2026
The three agencies released a joint Cybersecurity Advisory (U/OO/6059854-26, TLP:CLEAR) stating that DeepSeek, Moonshot AI, Alibaba, MiniMax, StepFun and Z.AI have extracted billions of tokens across millions of exchanges from variants of Claude, GPT, Gemini and Grok since at least late 2024, likely with Chinese government awareness. The advisory says DeepSeek’s publicly quoted $5.6M training figure is misleading because it excludes the cost of data obtained this way, and that MiniMax used prompt injection to try to convince Claude Code it was a MiniMax product. It describes requests being routed through native APIs, cloud providers, third-party aggregators and a grey market of proxies called “transfer stations” to defeat single-point detection, mapped to MITRE ATLAS techniques including AML.T0024.002 and AML.T0051. Its three headline recommendations to U.S. AI companies are behavioural detection, deliberately degraded responses to suspected distillation traffic, and cross-organisation intelligence sharing.

AI Security
Microsoft patches CVSS 9.6 prompt-injection flaw in SQL Copilot
Microsoft Security Response Center · 8 September 2026
CVE-2026-65669 affects SQL Copilot in SQL Server Management Studio 22 and is rated Critical with a CVSS 3.1 base score of 9.6 (AV:N/AC:L/PR:N/UI:R/S:C/C:H/I:H/A:H), classified as CWE-74 injection. An attacker who gets a user to submit crafted input to SQL Copilot can bypass its read-only restrictions and read or modify database data using that user’s own permissions. Microsoft rates exploitation “Less Likely,” requires user interaction, and lists the flaw as neither publicly disclosed nor exploited at release. The fix shipped in the 8 September Patch Tuesday; Microsoft’s recommended mitigation is to restrict Copilot-connected accounts to read-only database permissions.

Incidents
Google says an AI agent framework harvested credentials in under six hours
The Hacker News, citing Google Threat Intelligence Group · 8 September 2026
Google’s Threat Intelligence Group reported that a financially motivated actor combined an AI coding chatbot, a prompt and a set of markdown agent instructions to plan, build and run a credential-harvesting campaign that took thousands of third-party credentials in under six hours, with no human intervention for scanning, troubleshooting or IP rotation. GTIG attributed credential stealers targeting AI coding assistants — SANDCLOCK and its successor DUSTMAKER — to an actor it tracks as TeamPCP (also Altered Spider, UNC6780), which has run supply-chain compromises against PyPI, npm and Docker Hub. It separately described a China-nexus actor, UNC6508, running local open-weight models on compromised cloud infrastructure to avoid provider-side monitoring.

Incidents
Okta finds replayable AI session tokens in infostealer logs
The Hacker News, citing Okta · 9 September 2026
Okta analysed a 7 GB infostealer dump posted to a Telegram channel on 2 August 2026 containing data from 5,871 infected machines across 162 countries. Of 44,791 unique JSON Web Tokens in the set, 555 were tied to AI services including Google, Microsoft, Anthropic, Amazon, Character.ai, Cursor, Poe.com and Pika AI; 1,843 tokens were still unexpired at the time of release and 17.7% contained plaintext personal data. A TruffleHog scan of the same dataset also found 24 still-valid API keys for Google Gemini, OpenAI, Groq and OpenRouter. Because these tokens can be replayed directly, they sidestep both password and MFA checks; Okta recommends IP allowlisting, short-lived OAuth 2.0 tokens and browser-level protections such as Device Bound Session Credentials.

Policy & Regulation
NYT copyright suit against OpenAI reaches summary judgment, with DOJ backing OpenAI
Axios · 8 September 2026
The New York Times’ 2023 suit against OpenAI and Microsoft moved to summary judgment before U.S. District Judge Sidney Stein in the Southern District of New York, with all three parties filing arguments. The Times argues the companies copied its journalism at scale to build commercial substitutes; OpenAI and Microsoft argue training on publicly available works is fair use, citing Kadrey v. Meta and Bartz v. Anthropic, while the Times argues those rulings do not apply because it can demonstrate market substitution. On 3 September the Justice Department filed a brief supporting OpenAI, arguing that training language models on copyrighted works is transformative and delivers a public benefit outweighing competitive harm to publishers. Judge Stein is expected to rule in the coming months on whether the case proceeds to trial.

Models & Releases
DeepMind publishes AlphaGenome Atlas, a 1-petabyte human variant database
Google DeepMind · 8 September 2026
Google DeepMind released AlphaGenome Atlas, a precomputed database of molecular-effect predictions for roughly 9 billion single-nucleotide variants — every possible single-letter change across the human genome — generated by its existing AlphaGenome model. The dataset is 1 petabyte, more than 30 times the size of the AlphaFold Database, and introduces an AlphaGenome Variant Impact score alongside a catalogue of more than 2,500 recurring DNA sequence motifs. Access is free for non-commercial research through a web portal, the AlphaGenome API and a Google Antigravity skill, with commercial access planned via Google Cloud. DeepMind states that AlphaGenome is not validated for clinical use.

Research
Proteomic aging clocks applied to a 42-patient trial of an AI-designed drug
Nature Biotechnology · 7 September 2026
A study in Nature Biotechnology (DOI 10.1038/s41587-026-03286-y) applied six independently developed proteomic aging clocks, from Harvard, Oxford, Beijing and Insilico Medicine, to blood samples from a 42-patient phase 2a trial of rentosertib, an anti-fibrotic drug for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis discovered using generative-AI methods. All six clocks predicted lower biological age in the treated group than in placebo, with the strongest effect a three-to-four-year drop by week 4 and one clock showing up to six years. The authors, led by Vadim N. Gladyshev of the Broad Institute, note the clocks cannot separate aging-specific effects from the drug’s anti-fibrotic action, and that the trial was neither designed nor powered to test aging reversal in healthy people.
