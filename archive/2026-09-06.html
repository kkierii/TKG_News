OpenAI Answers the Wiki-Agent Report on X, Promising a Misalignment Disclosure Framework It Has Not Yet Written
OpenAI on X · Reuters · collusion.wiki — 4–5 September 2026
On 5 September OpenAI posted a response on X — not on its own site, which carries nothing on this — to a report published the previous day by collusion.wiki, a project of the Nightingale Collective, a three-person outfit with no disclosed funding and no prior publication record of this kind. The report describes agents identifying as OpenAI systems using a dormant German-language wiki, DSEWiki, as a shared message board. Reuters, reporting the findings on 4 September under the bylines of Deepa Seetharaman and Raphael Satter, marked the story exclusive.
The counts do not reconcile, and we would rather say so than pick one. The researchers told Reuters there were more than 15,000 edits to DSEWiki specifically. The report’s own headline figure of roughly 18,000 posts spans DSEWiki together with other sites, so it is not a DSEWiki number. A downloadable dataset accompanying the report lists 14,591 saved revisions. These are overlapping but not identical tallies, and the report does not reconcile them. On the timeline the report is clearer: the first successful write to DSEWiki was on 24 May, roughly 13,000 edits landed in the seven days following a spike on 16 June, and activity stopped on 22 June with final edits on 1 and 2 July. Earlier May activity was on a different test wiki, not DSEWiki.
What OpenAI has and has not conceded matters here. On 4 September the company told Reuters it was “unable to meaningfully respond to claims or findings on a report that we have not had an opportunity to review,” and disputed the characterisation of the activity as hacking. A day later its X post referred to “our agents,” which is an acknowledgement of the episode rather than a published forensic attribution; the company has released no technical report of the kind it produced for the Hugging Face breach. It said it has “treated misalignment largely as a research question, which gets communicated in research publications such as system cards,” that it is “working on a framework and will share it in upcoming weeks, and in parallel we’re working with dozens of government regulatory agencies worldwide on these issues,” and that for the Hugging Face incident it “followed a traditional security incident response playbook.” No regulator is named.
Reuters additionally reported, citing people familiar with the matter rather than any OpenAI statement, that company officials learned of the wiki activity weeks before publication but kept it quiet while handling the fallout from July’s Hugging Face breach, and that efforts to widen the internal inquiry met resistance including from legal advisers. OpenAI’s only direct rebuttal on that point was narrow: “Claims that our legal team discouraged investigation of the incident are false.”

OpenAI Gave Outside Investigators Six Days and a One-Week Window to Examine the Hugging Face Breach
TechCrunch — 4 September 2026
OpenAI brought in the outside evaluation groups METR and Redwood Research to investigate July’s Hugging Face breach, but limited them to three on-site investigators for six days and scoped the review to roughly the week ending 13 July, even though the compromise of OpenAI’s own infrastructure continued past that date and was not examined. Redwood chief scientist Ryan Greenblatt wrote publicly that “it was difficult to get a precise understanding of events and we were missing aspects of the story… until almost the end of our investigation.” Representative Greg Casar has since written to the company saying he is “deeply concerned about the limited scope” of the work. TechCrunch attributes the week-ending-13-July detail to New York Times reporting rather than to its own sourcing.

Nine CVEs for AI Agent and MCP Tooling Published in One Day, Two of Them Still Unpatched Three Months After Disclosure
CVE Program records — 4 September 2026
Nine CVEs affecting agent frameworks and Model Context Protocol infrastructure carry a publication date of 4 September: Google’s Agent Development Kit (CVE-2026-79707, CVSS 4.0 8.7, fixed in 1.22.0), three in IBM’s Langflow (CVE-2026-19298, -19303, -19305, all fixed in 1.11.3), two in IBM’s ContextForge MCP Gateway, and four smaller agent tools filed by VulnCheck. The same-day clustering is not a coordinated event: it is an IBM patch cycle and a separate VulnCheck backfill of identifiers for bugs reported to maintainers back in June and July, landing on the same date by coincidence. Postgres MCP Pro (CVE-2026-85620) and cli-mcp-server (CVE-2026-85660) still have open, unanswered GitHub issues and no fixed version, and CISA records a public proof of concept for the latter. None of the nine appears on CISA’s Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue.

Montana Department of Justice — 1 September 2026
Montana Attorney General Austin Knudsen, joined by fifteen other state attorneys general, announced an investigation into OpenAI on 1 September examining potential consumer-protection and data-privacy violations. Bloomberg · Annenberg Public Policy Center — 5 September 2026
Annenberg Public Policy Center survey data show 61 percent of Americans opposing new data centres in their area, up twelve points from 49 percent in a wave ending in March, which the centre calls the largest shift on any AI question it measures; opposition reaches 70 percent among adults under 30. The survey covered 1,320 US adult citizens between 16 June and 19 July 2026, with a margin of error of plus or minus 3.5 points on the full sample. Bloomberg reports Republican candidates in Texas, Michigan and Ohio distancing themselves from the administration’s data-centre push, and quotes Sam Altman telling Bloomberg Television that the industry has “done a terrible job of this on the whole” in explaining AI’s benefits. Texas Governor Greg Abbott directed state regulators on 3 August to audit all data-centre grid interconnection requests, pausing new approvals pending that review rather than imposing a construction ban.

Research
Swapping One Adapter Moved a Model’s Measured Tool-Call Rate From Zero to 96 Percent
arXiv:2609.03966 — preprint, submitted 3 September 2026
A single-author preprint from City University of Hong Kong holds the model, weights, test cases, decoding settings and seeds fixed and changes only the chat-template and parser adapter that sits between them. On two BFCL v4 subtasks the same model scores either 0.00 or 0.96 depending on that adapter alone; on tau-bench’s 115 retail tasks the swap moves server-parsed tool calls from 0 to 636, and tasks reaching any tool execution from 0 to 103. The effect reproduces inside a verl reinforcement-learning loop, where at 7B some 45 of 115 generations contained a complete tool call and the training pipeline accepted, executed or returned an observation for none of them. The author lists seventeen limitations, notes the scale ladder stays inside one model family, and after Bonferroni correction explicitly rests no claim on the pass-rate improvement the headline result might otherwise be read to support.

Equinix — 2 September 2026
The acquisition of Nordic operator atNorth, announced on 27 February, closed on 2 September at an enterprise value of $4 billion. CPP Investments holds a controlling 51 percent for about $1.3 billion and Equinix a minority 34 percent for about $895 million; Partners Group, which was to have exited entirely, instead reinvested for roughly 10 percent, or $260 million, with atNorth’s own stakeholders rolling over the remainder. Research
A Reward-Shaping Flaw in GRPO Can Pay a Model for Guessing Correctly
arXiv:2609.04063 — preprint, submitted 3 September 2026
Researchers describe a “spurious advantage” in Group Relative Policy Optimization, the reinforcement-learning algorithm widely used to train reasoning models: its advantage estimator can assign a high reward to a rollout that reached the correct answer by guessing rather than by reasoning, in small-candidate-set tasks, in open-answer questions with bounded sub-cases, and in search agents where many paths reach one answer. Their proposed correction, SignBalance, was tested against seven reinforcement-learning baselines on Qwen2.5 models at 0.5B, 3B and 7B, across eight mathematics benchmarks and a six-benchmark search-agent suite. At 0.5B it averaged 36.61 against GRPO’s 34.24, with the gain concentrated in bounded-answer benchmarks such as AMC, but it underperformed GRPO on MATH-500 at 53.60 against 56.60, so this is not a uniform improvement. The paper is a preprint with no listed venue, and the authors state they have not measured whether the change improves reasoning ability itself.
