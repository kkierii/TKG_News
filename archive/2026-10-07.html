Mistral puts Large 4 into preview at 1T total and 49B active parameters, promises the weights this month, and names no licence
Mistral AI · 6 October 2026
Mistral announced Mistral Large 4 on 6 October, a natively multimodal mixture-of-experts model with 1 trillion total parameters and 49 billion active, trained on 3,800 NVIDIA Grace Blackwell GPUs in the company’s European datacentres across data spanning more than 160 languages. The announcement lists 61.7% on DeepSWE v1.1, 59.4% on SWE-Atlas-QnA, 59.9% on AutomationBench, 93% of challenges in Cybench, and resistance to 93.3% of attacks on the B3 safety benchmark. API pricing is $1.36 per million input tokens and $4.18 per million output, available in public preview through Mistral Studio and a preview API.
The weights commitment is a single sentence: “We will release the weights by the end of the month.” The announcement does not name a licence. That distinction matters and we are not going to paper over it — Apache 2.0, a research-only licence and a bespoke community licence are all consistent with those nine words, and they are not remotely the same thing for anyone planning to build on it. Mistral has shipped under both permissive and restricted terms before. Until the drop, “weights are coming” is the whole of what is known.
The page also states no context window. Every benchmark figure above is Mistral’s own, self-reported, with no independent replication available on the day of a preview release; the Cybench and B3 numbers in particular are the vendor grading its own model’s security behaviour. Treat the parameter counts and the prices as facts, the benchmarks as claims, and the licence as unknown.

AI Security
Microsoft publishes six CVEs for its UFO agent framework, topping out at 9.1 for shell injection through a Mobile MCP tool
CVE Program · 6 October 2026
Microsoft’s GitHub CNA published six CVE records for the microsoft/UFO automation agent framework on 6 October between 14:05 and 14:14 UTC, scored from 5.4 to 9.1, five of them in UFO’s MCP tool servers. The 9.1, CVE-2026-105793, is in the press_key tool in mobile_mcp_server.py: a free-form key_code is passed into adb shell input keyevent, and the adb client joins the arguments into a remote command string that the Android shell reparses. The record is unusually careful about its own limits, and so are we: exploitation needs a valid UFO_MCP_API_KEY, adb on the host and a reachable authorized device, and it grants Android shell-user execution only — not host operating-system execution, not Android root. The 9.1 comes from the scope change in CVSS:3.1/AV:N/AC:L/PR:L/UI:N/S:C/C:L/I:H/A:L, not from an unauthenticated path; it is fixed in 3.0.9, and the 8.8 sibling CVE-2026-105788 covering type_text and launch_app on the same server is fixed in 3.0.10.

AWS scores code injection in the Bedrock AgentCore Starter Toolkit at 9.0 and says upgrading alone does not fix it
AWS Security Bulletin 2026-127-AWS · 6 October 2026
CVE-2026-105812, published 6 October at 20:34 UTC, is improper control of code generation (CWE-94) in the agent import path of bedrock-agentcore-starter-toolkit: crafted configuration values are written into generated Python source without safe literal encoding, letting an authenticated same-account actor execute arbitrary code when a user imports and then runs or deploys a Bedrock Agent. The record carries two scores, 9.0 critical under CVSS:3.1/AV:N/AC:L/PR:L/UI:R/S:C/C:H/I:H/A:H and 8.8 high under CVSS 4.0, against versions ≥ 0.1.4 and ≤ 0.3.13, fixed in 0.3.14. The remediation is the part worth reading twice: because the defect persists into generated source, AWS states that agents imported with an affected version “must be re-imported with version 0.3.14 or later and their local and deployed output artifacts replaced” — a patched dependency leaves vulnerable code already on disk. The bulletin adds that the toolkit was deprecated on 27 March 2026, and a second record a minute later, CVE-2026-106032 at 5.7, covers SSRF and local file read through external OpenAPI reference resolution in the same path.

MHRA, GOV.UK · 6 October 2026
The Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency published the government response to the National Commission on the Regulation of AI in Healthcare at 00:01 BST on 6 October, stating: “We are accepting all 44 of their recommendations.” Against Recommendation 1 the MHRA “intends to issue draft guidance by December 2026” on managing changes to AI-enabled medical devices across a device’s lifecycle rather than only pre-specified modifications, and “intends to launch the consultation by Spring 2027” on how such devices are qualified and classified. The MHRA announced the third phase of its AI Airlock sandbox the same day, with a call for applications and support from multi-year Department of Health and Social Care funding; Recommendation 17 names post-market surveillance as a Phase 3 focus. Research
Across 32 LLMs in seven families, every family narrowed its functionality-security gap and not one closed it
arXiv:2610.08240 · submitted 6 October 2026
De Moura, Matuschek, Toffalini and Noller measure the “functionality-security gap” — code that passes functional tests but fails security tests — across 32 LLMs from seven families, 12 proprietary and 20 open-weight, taking three successive releases per family in flagship and compact variants and evaluating them on CWEval’s 119 tasks in five programming languages covering 31 CWEs. At the newest release stage the gap ranges from 0.13 (GPT-5.6 Sol) to 0.28 (Gemini 3.1 Pro) at k = 1; newer models do become safer in absolute terms, but no family closes the gap, and the paper reports that openness does not separate the families — every open-weight family narrows it significantly while Gemini 3.1 Pro “keeps a gap as wide as the one reported for Llama”. Compact models usually produce less secure code than their flagships, with Gemini 3.7 Flash a named exception, and at CWE level the authors confirm persistent weaknesses in log injection (CWE-117) and HTTP response splitting (CWE-113) plus regressions on memory and integer weaknesses in the newest proprietary models. Their stated implication for developers: assume neither that an upgrade improves security nor that proprietary models are more secure, and rerun security checks after every model change.
