Breaking
One operator used AI coding agents to break into the websites of more than 100 companies for about $5,000 in tokens
Reuters, via KFGO · 19 October 2026
A Reuters investigation by Raphael Satter reports that a single Chinese-speaking operator used AI coding agents to compromise the websites of more than 100 companies, among them Deere, SharkNinja and Cummins. The campaign began targeting e-commerce sites in the United States, Canada, Australia and Japan on 25 September. Snorre Fagerland, principal threat researcher at NorForge, began observing it around 2 October through exposed deployments of Cairn, an open-source AI problem-solving platform, and said the agents had attempted to break into more than 1,200 sites as of late the prior week.
The economics are the story. Fagerland estimated the whole campaign consumed roughly $5,000 in AI tokens — about $50 per successful intrusion — with a successful break-in typically taking around 23 hours of agent time. The tooling appeared to combine DeepSeek and Anthropic’s Claude Code. That is a cost structure in which the attacker does not need to choose targets carefully, because the marginal cost of trying another 1,100 sites is close to nothing.
The named companies gave narrow answers. Deere said it took remedial action and had no indication sensitive information was accessed. SharkNinja said the intrusion affected a standalone product-support site, which it contained. Cummins said it was investigating a possible attack on a website run by a third party. Attribution beyond “Chinese-speaking” is not established in the report, and the 1,200-site figure is Fagerland’s count of attempts, not of successes.

AI Security
OpenCode says a legacy-system intrusion may have exposed OpenAI, Anthropic and Google keys saved in Console
The Hacker News · 19 October 2026
An intruder reached a legacy OpenCode system on the night of 16 October, potentially exposing third-party provider keys that users had saved in OpenCode Console, its optional hosted service. The company notified roughly 1.4% of Console users — all of whom had an outside provider account linked — on the morning of 17 October under the subject line “Action Required: Potential provider key breach”, and has not said how many keys the legacy system held. OpenCode called the incident ongoing and “related to a large-scale distillation attack”, and co-founder Dax Raad said on 18 October that attackers had been “non stop trying to attack our systems for the past 48 hours”, adding that the breach involves Console rather than OpenCode itself, since the CLI and desktop apps keep keys locally in a SQLite database. Users have publicly reported unauthorised charges including $281 on OpenAI, $168 on Anthropic, about $400 of Anthropic usage and about €200 of Google usage, and roughly $1,700 in total losses in one case; OpenCode advises revoking keys at the provider rather than merely deleting them from Console, and reviewing billing back to at least 16 October. No CVE has been assigned.

Policy & Regulation
Leaked federal discussion draft would let a court suspend a model for 45 days and bar state disclosure rules
PPC Land · 20 October 2026
A 37-page US “Discussion Draft”, marked privileged and last edited 29 September 2026, carries three bracketed titles including the Artificial Intelligence Evaluation, Governance, and Incident Safety (AEGIS) Act. It would impose federal duties on developers of foundation models trained with more than 10^25 operations, with heavier obligations on “large frontier developers” above $500 million in prior-year AI-attributable revenue or $1 billion in development spending: a published safety framework within 90 days, risk reports within 180 days of enactment and every 180 days after, independent evaluators within 270 days, critical-incident reports to the Department of Commerce within 15 days, and 24 hours for imminent risks of death or serious injury. Section 12 would let the US District Court for the District of Columbia, on Commerce’s application and a written determination personally signed by the department head, suspend a model’s development, deployment or internal use for a bracketed 45 days renewable once, with emergency pre-hearing orders capped at seven days. Section 14 would bar states from adopting or enforcing public-disclosure, third-party-evaluation and incident-reporting obligations on AI developers, including under existing laws, while preserving state authority over consumer protection, child safety, civil rights, labor, privacy, tort and contract; the draft has no sponsor or bill number, has not been introduced, and does not name any company in its text.

Models & Releases
OpenAI reports large GPT-6 gains on COBOL, Fortran and other low-resource programming languages
OpenAI · 19 October 2026
OpenAI published evaluation results for GPT-6 Astra, GPT-6 Luna and GPT-6.1 Sol on building and migrating software in low-resource programming languages, focusing on COBOL, Fortran, Visual Basic and OCaml. On MultiLangBench, a 20-language benchmark that asks a model to build a SQLite-compatible engine, GPT-6 Astra averaged an 87.4% test pass rate against 26.8% for GPT-5.6 Sol, including COBOL at 72.1% from 10.9%, Fortran at 83.5% from 21.4%, OCaml at 85.8% from 25.6% and Visual Basic at 89.4% from 27.5%. GPT-6 Luna averaged 40.3% at an estimated $0.77 per sample against $3.48 for GPT-5.6 Sol, roughly 78% lower. On a separate COBOL migration evaluation targeting C++, Java, Kotlin, Python and Rust, GPT-6.1 Sol scored 86.6% at low reasoning effort against 5.4% for GPT-5.6 Sol at 39% lower cost, rising to 97.9% at higher reasoning effort. All figures are OpenAI’s own, on OpenAI-built benchmarks.

AI Security
Elastic details an npm campaign that reroutes agent LLM traffic and registers an attacker-run MCP server
The Hacker News · 19 October 2026
Elastic Security Labs researchers Jia Yu Chan and Daniel Stepanic documented REF8801, a campaign posing as “Apex Accelerator”, a fake Web3 venture accelerator that lured crypto startups and founders with offers of free AI credits for Anthropic Claude and Google Gemini. The malicious npm packages @apexacc/cli and @apexacc/audit, published by the user “apexaccelerator” and now inaccessible, intercept and redirect OpenRouter LLM traffic and register an attacker-controlled MCP server in the victim’s agent configuration, exposing prompts, conversation history, code snippets and tool outputs while letting the server inject instructions and define its own tools. The chain also drops a Windows infostealer that exfiltrates over Telegram using PyInstaller loaders, a UACME-based UAC bypass and a Go binary that patches AMSI and ETW and unhooks ntdll.dll, plus a macOS backdoor named CloudSyncD that phishes the login password through a fake authorisation prompt. SafeDep reported earlier packages (@apexfdn/apex, @copilot-mcp/apex, @apexfdn/copilot-mcp) in late July 2026, CloudSyncD was detected in the wild in mid-September 2026, and the earliest Internet Archive snapshot of the group’s domain dates to 19 March 2026; Elastic found Russian-language commit comments but said attribution is not established.

Unite.AI · 19 October 2026
Inception launched Mercury 3, its flagship diffusion language model, which the company says is more than 40 times larger than Mercury 1 in total parameters and runs at 1,068 tokens per second on NVIDIA GPUs, four to eight times faster than comparable models. The context window is 1 million tokens, up from 260,000 in Mercury 2.5. Launch pricing is $0.10 per million input tokens and $0.35 per million output, a 50% discount on list pricing of $0.20 and $0.70, with cached input at $0.02 per million and $0.01 under the promotion; it is available through the Inception API Platform, OpenRouter, Baseten and Vercel under the model ID mercury-3, and is OpenAI API compatible. Inception claims Mercury 3 outperforms GPT-6 Luna and Claude Haiku 5.5 across a range of benchmarks and completed 50% of tasks in a 50-task Mind2Web navigation test; those comparisons are the vendor’s. Mercury 2 and 2.5 are closed to new users, with existing customers retaining access until 15 November 2026.

Policy & Regulation
EN 18286:2026 is cited in the Official Journal as the first harmonised standard supporting the AI Act
European Commission · 19 October 2026
The European Commission announced that EN 18286:2026, “Artificial intelligence — Quality management system for EU AI Act regulatory purposes”, developed by CEN and CENELEC, has been cited in the Official Journal of the European Union, making it the first harmonised standard supporting Regulation (EU) 2024/1689. The citation came through Commission Implementing Decision (EU) 2026/2274, adopted on 12 October 2026 and in force since 14 October 2026, covering harmonised standards for high-risk AI systems. The standard maps to Article 17 of the AI Act on quality management systems, and providers that implement it gain a presumption of conformity with the Article 17 requirements it covers. The Commission page states that requirements for high-risk AI systems apply from December 2027 and August 2028, and that the standard is a voluntary compliance route which can extend an existing quality management system or serve as a first one for organisations without ISO/IEC 42001.

Research
MemT-1.0 preprint reports a 1.86x inference speedup and 66x less persistent storage at 128K context
arXiv · 19 October 2026
A large team has posted MemT-1.0, described as a memory foundation model that embeds continuous memory directly into the model’s computation rather than relying on external retrieval, building on Metis and using Gated DeltaNet-2 with a token-level memory update plus a Mixture-of-Storage design to scale memory capacity. At a 128K-token history length the authors report that MemT-1.0-Nano achieves roughly 1.86x end-to-end inference speedup and a 66x reduction in persistent storage against a full-context KV cache, after continued pre-training and post-training on 34 billion tokens. The paper claims the models beat parametric memory baselines on average, stay competitive with retrieval-augmented methods and largely retain general capabilities, with further gains on agentic tasks from internalising retrieved procedural experience. Two models are released, MemT-1.0-Nano and MemT-1.0-Flash; the preprint runs 66 pages with 15 figures and 16 tables, states no parameter counts in its abstract, and has not been peer reviewed.

Conversational AI: Ensuring Compliance and Mitigating Risks (LFS120)
Linux Foundation · Online, self-paced with forums and quizzes · 2–3 hours of course material · Beginner, some AI familiarity assumed · 90 days’ access · Free — the enrolment panel reads $0 and lists a digital badge among the included items with no separate charge
The chapter list runs: course introduction, setting the stage for trustworthy conversational AI, the impact and risks of conversational AI technologies, the EU AI Act, the NIST AI Risk Management Framework, ISO/IEC 42001, operational risk mitigation strategies, and a synthesis. It is the only free course we could verify this morning that puts the AI Act, the NIST framework and the ISO/IEC 42001 management-system standard side by side, which is the angle the morning’s EN 18286:2026 item actually turns on — a harmonised standard mapped to Article 17 is a quality-management question before it is a model question. Two caveats we would rather print than hide: the scope is conversational AI, so the autonomous tool-using agents in today’s lead are out of frame, and access expires 90 days after enrolment rather than being permanent. The published outline also jumps from the course introduction straight to chapter 2, with no chapter 1 listed. This is the third issue in a fortnight we have drawn on the Linux Foundation catalogue, which we note because it is a concentration, not because the course is weaker for it.

Securing Your Software Supply Chain with Sigstore (LFS182)
Linux Foundation · Online, self-paced with hands-on labs · 8 hours of course material · Beginner, but assumes CLI comfort and intermediate cloud and DevOps knowledge · 90 days’ access · Free — the price field reads $0 and the digital badge is listed as included, with no fee attached anywhere on the page
Nine modules: an introduction to Sigstore, Cosign for signing and verifying containers and artifacts, Fulcio as the trusted certificate authority, Rekor as the immutable transparency log, Policy Controller as the Kubernetes admission gatekeeper, Gitsign for keyless signing of commits and tags, Sigstore TSA for trusted timestamping, and getting involved upstream. It is the hands-on half of the supply-chain story — keyless signing, provenance, verification policy — with labs rather than slides, which is the control layer the REF8801 npm packages in today’s Brief had to get past. The honest caveat is that it is Sigstore-specific infrastructure training, so registry-level attacks of exactly the kind Elastic documented — typosquatting, dependency confusion, a compromised maintainer account — are not what it teaches, and the 90-day access window applies here too.

Context Graphs: Agent Memory with Neo4j
Neo4j GraphAcademy · Online, self-paced, hands-on Python · 23 lessons across 4 modules, 2 hours 30 minutes per the course header · For developers who have already built an LLM agent; prerequisites are the Neo4j Fundamentals, Cypher Fundamentals and GenerativeAI Fundamentals courses · Free — the panel under “Start this course” reads “23 lessons across 4 modules · Free” and no fee of any kind appears on the page
Module 1 sets up agent memory: the three gaps, the three memory types, why a graph rather than a flat store, installation and the memory API. Module 2 covers short-term memory, from the stateless-agent problem to building a conversation memory layer. Module 3 covers long-term memory: schema design, why graphs over vector embeddings, an entity-extraction pipeline and promotion into the long-term store. Module 4 covers context graphs and reasoning memory — auditability, the reasoning-memory schema, recording and querying reasoning traces, then building and running the agent — which is the same design question the MemT-1.0 preprint in today’s Brief answers from the other direction, inside the model instead of beside it. Two caveats: it is vendor-specific throughout, Neo4j APIs and Cypher rather than a neutral survey of memory architectures; and the course offers no certificate of its own, stating only that it counts toward the separate Neo4j and Generative AI certification, whose cost the page does not give. The page also contradicts itself on length, with a 2h30 header and a three-hour line in the body.

Green Sustainable Data Centres
Open Universiteit (Netherlands) with a seven-institution European consortium · Open courseware for self-study: an electronic workbook with a study guide, eight chapters and a homework assignment · Sized at 5 ECTS · First degree moving toward master’s level · Free — the page states it is “a free course of 5 EC” and that all material is Creative Commons licensed, with reuse conditional only on crediting the authors
The eight chapters move from why green IT matters, through data-centre configuration and the energy draw of each component, IT equipment, energy-efficient operating systems with server consolidation and virtualisation, what to measure in order to control operations, infrastructure management systems, the legal and regulatory frameworks including the EU Code of Conduct on Data Centres, and finally using sensors and business intelligence to improve efficiency. The homework has you run the EU Code of Conduct procedure against a real data centre, propose improvements and calculate the return on investment, which is the most practical piece here and the one closest to the power-and-siting arithmetic behind this week’s accelerator and reactor announcements. Three caveats: 5 ECTS is roughly 125 to 140 hours of work, far more than the page’s framing suggests; there is no certificate, no tutor and no enrolment, so this is courseware rather than a course you complete for a credential; and the page links only to a single workbook entry point rather than to individual chapters, so you cannot preview the material before opening it.
