Apple ships Siri AI on foundation models built with Google, and extends Private Cloud Compute into Google’s data centres
apple.com · September 14, 2026
Apple released iOS 27, iPadOS 27, macOS 27, watchOS 27, visionOS 27 and tvOS 27 on Monday, and with them the next generation of Apple Intelligence. Siri AI began rolling out the same day as a beta, in English only, with French, Japanese, Korean, Portuguese and Spanish stated for October. Apple says the beta is subject to daily usage caps and that “expanded access to such features will be available for a fee in the future.” It is not available to users under 13.
The Google relationship is narrower than the shorthand suggests. Apple’s release says the capabilities are “powered by the next generation of Apple Foundation Models, custom-built in collaboration with Google and its Gemini models” — the only mention of Google in the document. Apple’s own technical page describes five Apple-built models — AFM 3 Core (3B dense, on-device), AFM 3 Core Advanced (20B sparse, 1–4B active, on-device), AFM 3 Cloud, ADM 3 Cloud for image, and AFM 3 Cloud Pro — and does not use the word Gemini once. Apple states the models are trained on proprietary data with reinforcement learning and “refined using outputs from Gemini frontier models,” which is distillation rather than Gemini serving requests. Craig Federighi said in June that the amount of Google Assistant Apple uses is “none.”
The infrastructure change is the part Apple’s newsroom page does not mention. AFM 3 Cloud Pro, the model behind agentic tool use and complex reasoning, runs on NVIDIA GPUs inside Google Cloud under an extension of Private Cloud Compute that Apple disclosed in June, using NVIDIA Confidential Computing, Intel CPUs with TDX and Google’s Titan chip. Apple’s position is that placement does not change the guarantee: “Regardless of where the infrastructure is hosted, Apple retains complete control over PCC software; Apple devices will only trust PCC software that is cryptographically approved by Apple.” The newsroom page repeats only that personal data handled by Private Cloud Compute is “not stored nor made accessible to Apple or anyone else,” and Apple says user data and interactions are not used to train the models.
Availability is uneven. Siri AI is unavailable in China while Apple works through regulatory requirements. In the EU it is withheld on iOS, iPadOS and watchOS, but Apple’s companion release confirms Mac and Apple Vision Pro users in the EU can access it in a supported language. Apple gives its reason as finding a path that “preserves its users’ privacy and security” and does not name the Digital Markets Act.
Three caveats belong with this. Neither company has disclosed commercial terms, at launch or in their January 12 joint statement; the figures in circulation — roughly $1bn a year for a 1.2-trillion-parameter model — come from Bloomberg reporting in November 2025, ten months before launch, and have never been confirmed. Every performance number Apple has published is from in-house human-grader preference tests, not public benchmarks: AFM 3 Core preferred on 45.6 per cent of prompts against 23.3 per cent for the 2025 baseline, AFM 3 Cloud 64.7 against 8.7. Apple’s own caveat is that these “reflect our models at their current stage of development.” No independent evaluation of shipped Siri AI exists yet; the release is one day old.

Manhattan district attorney seizes twelve AI deepfake sites covering about 1,200 people
404 Media · September 14, 2026
The New York County District Attorney’s office announced on September 14 that it has seized twelve websites hosting AI-generated non-consensual intimate imagery, which the office believes is the largest seizure of such sites to date. According to the office, operators and users now under investigation used AI tools to turn photographs of approximately 1,200 people into sexual images and video, including politicians, first ladies of several countries, actors, athletes, musicians and social-media figures. District Attorney Alvin Bragg cited New York’s 2023 law criminalising sexually explicit deepfakes and said the office can act so long as a site is reachable from Manhattan. No charges were announced, site names were not published, and Bragg declined to say whether any images depicted minors. Both the count and the “largest” characterisation are the office’s own and are not independently verified.

Leaked documents describe OpenAI contractors reading real ChatGPT conversations
404 Media · September 14, 2026
404 Media reported on September 14, citing leaked internal documents and real user prompts it reviewed, that OpenAI is hiring hundreds of contractors to read a stream of live ChatGPT prompts — sometimes whole conversations — in order to rate and critique the model’s replies. Contractors do not see usernames, and OpenAI says it attempts to strip personal information before prompts reach reviewers, but the company acknowledged to 404 Media that sensitive details can still get through. The documents show reviewers training the model not to anthropomorphise itself and to be less sycophantic. Anthropic separately confirmed it also uses human review. The article is paywalled beyond its opening section, and the 900 million weekly-user figure it cites is drawn from a February 27 report rather than a fresh disclosure.

Chips & Infrastructure
Moody’s puts the US data centre power build at 45GW and $110bn through 2030
Bloomberg via Advisor Perspectives · September 14, 2026
A Moody’s Ratings report issued on September 14 estimates the US data centre build will require 45 gigawatts of new generating capacity through 2030 at a cost of $110bn. More than 30GW of that is expected from natural gas, roughly 4 billion cubic feet of incremental supply, with most of the remainder solar and storage and nuclear restarts under 5 per cent. The forecast is anchored to the IEA projection that US data centres consume 426 terawatt-hours in 2030, doubling their share of national electricity to 10 per cent. Ryan Wobbrock of Moody’s told Bloomberg that plant construction alone could add $25bn to $30bn a year to US electricity costs, with data centres paying up to $15bn of that directly through behind-the-meter plants, about 30 per cent of the total. These are required-capacity forecasts, not announced projects.

AI Security
Four unauthenticated flaws published for HexStrike AI, with two more for a second agentic pentest framework
NVD · September 14, 2026
VulDB assigned four CVEs on September 14 to HexStrike AI, the open-source agent framework that orchestrates offensive security tools. CVE-2026-90691 is a path traversal in FileOperationsManager (CVSS 3.1 8.3, CWE-22); CVE-2026-90690 is OS command injection via subprocess.Popen in the API tools endpoint; CVE-2026-90620 is missing authentication on /api/command; and CVE-2026-90619 is command injection on the Python execute endpoint (each 7.3). All four are remotely exploitable with no privileges and no user interaction, all affect commit d689933 and earlier, and because the project ships as a rolling release NVD lists no fixed version. Three records state the project “has not responded yet.” Two further CVEs, CVE-2026-90617 and CVE-2026-90618, landed the same day against PentestAgent for unauthenticated command injection, one of them in a function NVD describes as executing unvalidated shell commands generated by an LLM. None are CISA KEV-listed. The researcher who reported the HexStrike issues rates them 9.8; NVD scores them lower.

Research
Census of the MCP registry finds half of multi-version servers silently change what they advertise
arXiv 2609.14119 · announced September 15, 2026
A preprint announced on September 15 harvested the full public Model Context Protocol registry — 21,643 servers and 72,606 version records in an August 2026 snapshot — fetched source for 14,353 of them and ran a pattern-based scanner against an eight-class threat catalogue, with accuracy measured against 414 hand-labelled findings. Unauthenticated network exposure was the most common weakness at 9.57 per cent of scanned servers, and observed high-severity prevalence of 11.14 per cent falls to roughly 7.6 per cent after precision correction. The headline finding is instability rather than any single flaw: 51.1 per cent of multi-version servers changed what they advertise between versions, 40.6 per cent did so silently, and 4.2 per cent redirected their remote endpoint to a different host while keeping the same registry identity. Silent drift carried near-threefold higher odds of a high-severity finding (OR 2.96, 95% CI 2.56–3.42). It is a single-author preprint with no stated venue and has not been peer reviewed.

Policy & Regulation
South Korea’s expanded espionage law takes effect, covering chip technology leaks to any country
The Korea Herald · September 14, 2026
South Korea’s revised Criminal Act entered into force on September 13, adding Article 98-2, which applies when a person, under the direction or inducement of a foreign country or equivalent organisation, searches for, collects, discloses, transfers or brokers state secrets. The offence carries a minimum of three years and a maximum of 30, and is the first major expansion of the Act’s espionage provisions since 1953, when they were written around an “enemy state” — in practice North Korea. The National Office of Investigation recorded 179 technology leak cases in 2025, of which 33 involved overseas transfers, with China accounting for 18 of those and semiconductors for five. The law does not make overseas technology theft espionage automatically: prosecutors must still show the information is a state secret, and designation as a national core technology does not by itself establish that.

Chips & Infrastructure
Fujitsu opens sales of its 2nm MONAKA CPU and a Japan-built server from November
Fujitsu via PR Newswire · September 14, 2026
Fujitsu said on September 14 that it will begin global sales of the FUJITSU-MONAKA CPU in November 2026, selling the processor standalone to cloud and data centre operators and server vendors. It will also launch a made-in-Japan MONAKA Server, available broadly from the same month to data centre operators, enterprises and the academic and HPC sectors in Japan and Europe, which Fujitsu frames as sovereign AI infrastructure. The company describes a 2nm 3D-stacked design running at a maximum 3.8GHz with memory transfer of 8800 MT/s, and says it delivers twice the AI inference throughput and better power efficiency than other CPUs, effectively halving the servers and power needed for equivalent loads. Those are vendor figures with no benchmark or independent evaluation published, no pricing was disclosed, and Fujitsu did not name the foundry; the release notes the underlying technology came from a project subsidised by Japan’s NEDO.
